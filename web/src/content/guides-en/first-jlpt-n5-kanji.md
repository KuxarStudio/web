Once you write hiragana and katakana comfortably, it is time for kanji. This is the longest part of [learning to write Japanese by hand](/en/guides/learn-to-write-japanese-by-hand/), but the first step (level N5) is very manageable if you go in order.

## How many N5 kanji are there?

It depends who you ask: different lists mention about 100, 103 or 109 characters. The reason is that **the JLPT does not publish an official kanji list** per level, so every resource builds its own. Keep the idea: it is around a hundred, and what matters is learning the most basic, everyday ones, not matching a specific list.

## Which ones to start with

The simplest and most useful are usually:

| Group | Kanji | Strokes |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers | 一 二 三 四 五 六 七 八 九 十 | 1, 2, 3, 5, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2 |
| Days | 日 月 火 水 木 金 土 | 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 8, 3 |
| Basics | 人 大 小 山 川 | 2, 3, 3, 3, 3 |

Starting with numbers and days gives you characters with few strokes that are very frequent, and they also let you practice [the stroke order rules](/en/guides/japanese-stroke-order-rules/): 三 (top to bottom), 川 (left to right), 十 (horizontal before vertical), 小 (center, then sides).

## Learn components, not pictures

Many kanji are built from pieces you already know. For example, 休 ("to rest") is a person (人) next to a tree (木), and 本 is a 木 with a line at its base. Recognizing the pieces makes each new kanji easier to remember and to trace.

## A method that works

1. Learn **few kanji per session**, in meaningful groups (numbers, days, nature).
2. Trace following the order, then write from memory, as in [the practice loop](/en/guides/how-to-learn-hiragana-by-writing/).
3. Review the previous days' kanji before adding new ones.
4. Learn the readings **after** you can write the shape, or in parallel, but do not drop the writing.

## Is it worth writing them by hand?

Yes, even if you will not write them every day: writing a kanji forces you to notice every stroke and component, and that improves recognition. Some small-scale research points the same way: in a study by Longcamp and colleagues published in *Human Movement Science*, 12 adults learned new characters, and those who wrote them by hand recognized them better and confused them less often with their mirror image than those who typed them. It is a small sample, but it fits the experience of many Japanese learners. [Source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945706000649).
