Learning hiragana by writing it, not just looking at it, is the foundation of [writing Japanese by hand](/en/guides/learn-to-write-japanese-by-hand/). There is no magic timeline (everyone moves at their own pace), but there is an order that avoids getting stuck.

## Step 1: study by rows

Hiragana is organized in rows by sound. A common order is:

1. あ い う え お
2. か き く け こ
3. さ し す せ そ
4. た ち つ て と
5. な に ぬ ね の
6. は ひ ふ へ ほ
7. ま み む め も
8. や ゆ よ
9. ら り る れ ろ
10. わ を ん

Add **one row at a time** (or half a row if that is hard) and do not move on until the previous one comes out without looking.

## Step 2: the three-pass loop

For each character:

1. **Look** at the stroke order and the direction of each stroke.
2. **Trace** it following that order, a few times.
3. **Write it from memory** and compare.

If you hesitate on the third pass, go back to the first: it is a sign that character is not fixed yet. The general rules of order are in [the stroke order rules](/en/guides/japanese-stroke-order-rules/).

## Step 3: review before moving on

Before starting a new row, write the previous one from memory (and some from days ago). A few spaced repetitions work better than one long session you never review.

## Step 4: watch the look-alikes

Some pairs and trios get mixed up every time: ぬ and め, わ and れ and ね, る and ろ, は and ほ. Give them a separate review; we explain them in [similar-looking kana](/en/guides/similar-looking-kana/).

## Step 5: add the marks

Once you have the 46 basic characters down, learn the marks ゛ and ゜ (か → が, は → ぱ) and the small characters ゃ, ゅ and ょ. They are not new characters: they change the sound of the ones you already know.

## A few useful habits

- **Short, frequent sessions**: a little every day beats one whole afternoon.
- **Say the sound out loud** while you trace.
- **Alternate phone and paper**: see [phone or paper](/en/guides/practice-japanese-writing-phone-or-paper/).
